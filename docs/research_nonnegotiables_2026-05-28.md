# Research Non-Negotiables - 2026-05-28

This file exists so that future me cannot quietly drift back into work I already know I hate.

If I ever become successful enough to publish anywhere, I still do not want to build my research identity on values I do not respect.

## The two things I currently hate most

### 1. Discriminating against work that has no wet-lab experiments

I do not accept the idea that a paper becomes automatically more serious, more intelligent, or more worthy just because it contains wet-lab validation.

Wet-lab evidence is one kind of evidence. It is not a moral credential.

Bad theory with wet-lab is still bad theory.  
Weak algorithms with wet-lab are still weak algorithms.  
Unclear problem definitions do not become deep just because mice or materials appear at the end.

What I want instead:

- Judge work by clarity of the question.
- Judge work by rigor of the method.
- Judge work by whether the evidence actually matches the claim.
- Treat wet-lab as one validation channel, not as an automatic hierarchy over computational work.

My standard:

> No paper should be respected just because it is expensive to run.

## 2. Calling a stitched-together pile of ordinary models an algorithm

I hate the pattern:

```text
take a random forest
+ add a simulator
+ add a calibrator
+ add a heuristic
+ add a post-hoc plot
= claim a novel algorithm
```

That is not a real algorithmic contribution unless there is a single governing object behind it:

- one formal problem,
- one central inference object,
- one clear optimization or construction principle,
- one reason the pieces belong together.

If the method is only a workflow or a stack of tools, then I should call it a pipeline, framework, or benchmark system, not pretend it is a new algorithm.

My standard:

> If I cannot state the core object in one sentence, then I do not yet have an algorithm.

## What this means for my own work

If I write algorithm papers, I want them to be centered on things like:

- posterior family inference under partial observation,
- identifiable vs feasible directions in mechanistic systems,
- uncertainty under sparse data and distribution shift,
- active state inference in dynamical systems,
- closed-loop scientific decision-making.

Drug release, PLGA, chitosan, liposomes, or hydrogels can be testbeds.
They should not be cages.

## JCR

I am writing this down clearly:

I do not want my long-term research taste to be shaped by the aesthetics of `Journal of Controlled Release`.

That does not mean every paper in JCR is bad.  
It means I do not want to optimize my best ideas for a venue whose center of gravity keeps pulling the work back toward:

- formulation-factor storytelling,
- release-curve incrementalism,
- weak mechanism language,
- and application-first framing of what are actually algorithmic or scientific questions.

If future me is tempted to submit there because it is convenient, easier, or culturally expected, stop and ask:

1. Is this actually a release paper, or am I shrinking an algorithmic idea into a release paper?
2. Am I using the domain as a real scientific testbed, or am I letting the venue define the ambition?
3. Would I still be proud of this framing five years later?

If the honest answer is bad, do not submit there.

## Positive rule

I do not need to reject wet-lab work.
I do not need to reject applications.
I do need to reject confused standards.

The goal is:

- respect real evidence,
- demand real algorithmic clarity,
- avoid fake novelty,
- and refuse to confuse domain habit with intellectual necessity.

## Final reminder to future me

Do not build a career around papers that make me feel smaller than the ideas really are.

Use the domain.
Do not let the domain domesticate the question.
